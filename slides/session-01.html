Part 1 · What is this machine?

One sentence that explains most of the failures you just saw

Part 1 answers one question: what is this machine? A single sentence about how an LLM works explains most of what the demo showed: the cut-off JSON, the empty promise and the unchecked refund.

Today: one API call and a paragraph of instructions. An afternoon.

What didn't

Left card: building an AI feature got much easier. In 2019, sorting tickets into categories meant labelling thousands of examples, training your own model and keeping specialists around to maintain it. Now you write instructions in plain English and call an API.

Right card: running it safely did not get easier. The model is a remote dependency that can be slow, down, expensive or wrong. Notice that v1 failed on this card, not the left one.

Who has integrated a payment API, and what made it safe? Timeouts, retries, idempotency keys (so a retry can't charge twice), logging and checking the response. That same toolkit is what this course applies to a dependency that is sometimes wrong.

The one sentenceAn LLM predicts the next word. That's it.

An LLM (large language model) is a program trained on a huge amount of text. Given some text, it scores every possible next word, picks one, and repeats. Like your phone's autocomplete, scaled up enormously.

This is the one sentence the session rests on. The model looks at the text so far, gives every possible next piece a score (a probability), picks one, appends it, and repeats. Try it on your phone: type "I want a" in a messaging app and read the suggestions. An LLM is the same idea, trained on vastly more text and much better at using context.

Two details. The percentages in the diagram are illustrative, and "word" is a simplification: it really predicts tokens. And there is no database inside. The model isn't looking anything up; it only has patterns learned from text. That's why likely-sounding and true are not the same thing.

The one sentenceWatch it write, one piece at a time

Every reply is written one piece at a time. That's why answers appear word by word, and why long answers take longer.

The model writes one piece at a time (the pieces are called tokens, slide 14). At each step it looks at the whole text so far, including what it just wrote, which is the fast part. Then it adds one more piece and goes again until it predicts "end". Two practical consequences:

  • Streaming: because the pieces exist one at a time, an app can show them as they arrive. That's why ChatGPT seems to type at you, and why v1's raw text appeared live on screen.
  • Long answers are slow answers: every extra token is another trip around the loop, so the length of the output drives how long you wait. We measure this later today.

TokensWhat the model actually reads

A token is a piece of text: a word, part of a word, or a symbol. Models read, write and charge in tokens. 1 token ≈ ¾ of an English word.

Capitals, prices and IDs cost more: REFUND is 2 tokens, $24.99 is 4.

Models never see letters, only tokens. Seven words became ten tokens here, and the order ID alone is four (O, -, 482, 91). Common lowercase words are usually one token; capitals, prices, dates and IDs split into more. Why you care:

  • Money: you're billed per token.
  • Speed: output is written one token at a time.
  • Space: the context window (next slide) is measured in tokens.

Because it sees pieces, not letters, a model is bad at things like counting the r's in "strawberry", and you should never trust it with exact arithmetic on prices. Do maths in code. These counts come from the tokenizer used by GPT-4o and GPT-4.1; other models split text differently.

The model's deskThe context window: everything the model sees in one call

The context window is everything the model can see in one call: instructions, documents, the conversation, and the answer it's writing.

A desk: a fixed size, wiped after every call, and you pay for everything on it.

The whole desk: 2,431 tokens. The customer's message: 47 of them, about 2%. The other 98% is instructions and policies, paid for on every ticket.

gpt-4.1-mini's desk holds about 1 million tokens, about eight 300-page books. gpt-4o-mini's holds 128,000.

The context window is the model's working space for one call. On the desk: instructions, documents, the conversation and the answer it's writing. The desk has a size limit, it's wiped after each call, and you pay for everything on it.

The real numbers for v1 on T-1001: 2,431 tokens in the prompt, and only 47 are the customer's words. The six policy files alone are 2,329 tokens. So about 98% of what v1 pays for is text sent "just in case".

What earns its place on the desk? Only what this ticket needs. More isn't smarter: models often miss facts buried in the middle of long input, so stuffing everything in costs more and can answer worse.

Nine short messages, so why pay for almost 29,000 tokens? Every turn pays again for everything before it. That's incident #7: nobody wrote a bug, the cost simply grows with the conversation.

TemperatureHow the next token gets picked

Temperature is a number you send with each call: how random the pick is. Below, the model is finishing:
Resolution: issue a ___

Deskhand v1 uses temperature=1.0: the same ticket gets different answers. That's incident #8.

Think of weighted dice. The model has odds for each next token; the middle panel (temperature 1.0) shows its raw odds. At 0 the dice always land on the top choice: refund, every time. Above 1 the odds even out, so unlikely options come up more often. Temperature doesn't make the model smarter or dumber, only more or less adventurous. The chart's numbers are illustrative.

v1 sends temperature=1.0, so ten runs of one ticket give roughly six refunds, two replacements and the odd apology. That's incident #8, and the cheapest fix in the course: one number. Two caveats: temperature 0 gives the same answer almost every time, not always, and some newer reasoning models don't accept a temperature at all.

TemperaturePick a temperature: 0 or ~1?

  1. Sort a ticket into refund / shipping / warranty.
  2. Pull the order ID out of a customer's message.
  3. Write 5 different subject lines for a "sorry for the delay" email campaign.
  4. Decide how much money to refund.

For each task, vote with your fingers: 0 or 1.

1: 0, because a program routes on the category, so it must be repeatable. 2: 0, because there is exactly one right order ID. 3: about 0.7 to 1.0, because you want variety and a human picks. 4: 0.

The rule of thumb behind all four: 0 whenever a program reads the output. Go higher only when you want variety for a human to choose from.

Number 4 is the trap. Yes, 0, but even at 0 the model shouldn't have the last word on money: code should check the amount against the order and the policy before anything is paid. Temperature 0 makes answers repeatable, and a repeated answer can still be wrong. That still helps, because a repeatable bug is one you can debug.

  • Good at: transforming text it was given (classify, extract, summarise, rewrite, spot an angry tone).
  • Fakes convincingly: facts about your business, exact maths, citations. Nimbus's refund policy says 14 days, yet a model can state "30 days" with full confidence.

Summarise a 20-message thread: good at. What's 15% of $79.99: fakes convincingly, so do the maths in code. Where is my parcel: can't, not without a lookup tool.

Part 2 · Cost, speed, and where the model sits

The limits that decide whether an AI feature is a product or a demo

Part 1 covered what the model does. Part 2 covers what each call costs, in money and in seconds, and where the call sits in your system. Those two numbers often decide whether an AI feature can ship or stays a demo.

CostThe arithmetic of one call

cost = input tokens  × input price      # what you send
     + output tokens × output price     # what the model writes; prices are per 1 million tokens
Deskhand v1, ticket T-1001, on gpt-4.1-mini Tokens Price per 1M Cost
Input: instructions + 6 policies + ticket 2,431 $0.40 $0.00097
Output: the JSON answer ~150 $1.60 $0.00024
One call ≈ $0.0012

Per token, output costs 4× input. Here input still costs more in total, because we send 16× more of it.

One call is a tenth of a cent. Next: how it becomes 90 cents.

Providers bill per token, with separate prices for input (what you send) and output (what the model writes), quoted per million tokens. It's a unit-price sum: 2,431 × $0.40 ÷ 1,000,000 = $0.00097 plus 150 × $1.60 ÷ 1,000,000 = $0.00024, about $0.0012 together.

Per token, output costs four times as much as input. This call still spends more on input ($0.00097 against $0.00024), because it sends 16 times more input tokens than it gets back. These are gpt-4.1-mini's prices from PRICE_TABLE in checkpoints/01-triage/llm.py; treat them as illustrative and check your provider's pricing page.

At 50,000 tickets a month that's about $60. Is cost a problem? Not at this size. The next slide shows how ordinary decisions multiply it.

CostHow a $0.001 call becomes a $0.90 resolution

Change Why teams do it Per ticket
Deskhand v1: 1 call, small model (the starting point) $0.0012
A bigger model (gpt-4.1) "answers are better" $0.006
An agent: 12 calls in a loop, each re-sending the growing conversation (~30,000 tokens) "so it can look things up" $0.77
1 run in 5 retried from the start "it's flaky" $0.92

$0.90 of tokens on a typical $12 order is a broken product, and every step looked reasonable. Measure cost per successful resolution, retries included.

Each row is a decision a sensible team makes, and each multiplies the bill:

  • gpt-4.1 instead of gpt-4.1-mini ($2 in, $8 out per million): 2,431 × 2/1M + 150 × 8/1M ≈ $0.006.
  • An agent: the model calls tools (functions your code runs for it, like an order lookup) in a loop, re-sending the growing conversation each time. 12 calls × 30,000 tokens × $2/1M = $0.72, plus 12 × 500 output tokens × $8/1M = $0.05: about $0.77.
  • Retrying 1 run in 5 from scratch multiplies by 1.2: $0.92.

So measure cost per successful resolution. If an attempt costs 1 cent and 60% succeed, each success costs 1 ÷ 0.6 ≈ 1.7 cents, and someone still handles the failures.

LatencyCalls take seconds, and some take much longer

Reading your prompt is fast: one pass. Writing is slow: about 100 tokens a second, one at a time.

p95 is the time that 95% of calls beat. With 20 calls: sort them, take the 19th. The average hides the slow tail: here one customer in 20 waits about twice the average.

Latency is how long a call takes, from sending the request to having the answer. LLM calls take seconds. Reading your prompt is quick, because the model takes it in one pass. Writing is the slow part: output comes out one token at a time, roughly 50 to 150 tokens a second depending on model and load (about 100 is a fair round number).

The chart is an example run of 20 calls. The average is about 2.0 s, but an average hides the slow tail. p95 (the 95th percentile) is the time 95% of calls beat: sort the 20 times and take the 19th, here 3.9 s. Like a commute that averages 25 minutes: on interview day you still leave 45 minutes early.

Set timeouts from the tail. A 3-second timeout chosen because "the average is 2 s" would fail about one call in ten.

LatencyMake it faster: which change helps most?

A Deskhand call takes about 4 seconds. Your manager wants 3. Which change helps most?

  1. Send only refund-policy.md instead of all six: the prompt drops from 2,431 to about 520 tokens.
  2. Ask for a short JSON answer and cap it with max_tokens: the answer drops from 150 to 50 tokens.
  3. Change temperature from 1.0 to 0.

Vote with your fingers: 1, 2 or 3. Then a second vote: which one saves the most money?

Fastest: option 2. Biggest money saving: option 1. Option 3 changes neither.

Speed: the model reads the prompt in one pass but writes the answer one token at a time. At about 100 tokens a second, 100 fewer output tokens saves about a second, while 1,900 fewer prompt tokens barely changes the time. max_tokens is the request setting that caps answer length.

Money: option 1 removes about 1,900 input tokens, 1,900 × $0.40 ÷ 1M ≈ $0.0008 a call. Option 2 saves 100 × $1.60 ÷ 1M ≈ $0.0002. Input tokens are cheap, but v1 sends a lot of them. Temperature changes which token gets picked, not how many or how fast. All timings here are illustrations; real speed depends on model and load.

ArchitectureWhere the LLM call sits

A payment provider. You never call it without a timeout, and you never write its response to the database unchecked. Same rules here, plus one: this API can return a confident wrong answer with a 200 OK.

This is the shape of every LLM feature: the customer, your service (where the request_id is created), the LLM API, a check on the answer, then the reply, with logs collected along the way. Everything you build lives in the green boxes. The model is one dependency in the middle: slow, paid, and able to fail.

Treat it like a payment provider: always call it with a timeout, and never write its response to your database unchecked. The extra rule: an LLM can return a confident wrong answer with a 200 OK, so a successful HTTP status says nothing about whether the content is right. v1 has neither the "check the answer" box nor the log box; the toolkit you already know (timeouts, retries, logging, validation) fills them.

HabitLog every call, starting today

A request_id is a parcel tracking number. Every log line for that request carries it, so you can follow one request through every step.

Your service makes up a request_id when a request arrives, and every log line for that request carries it, like a parcel tracking number. JSONL (JSON Lines) means one JSON object per line, appended to a file. No framework needed: build a dict, json.dumps it, append it to logs/runs.jsonl.

The payoff: a customer complains, you search for their ID (grep <id> logs/runs.jsonl, or findstr on Windows; the ID on the slide is a made-up example) and get the whole story of that run: model, token counts, cost, latency. Tonight · step 2Your first API call, done properly 

import json, time, uuid
from dotenv import load_dotenv
from openai import OpenAI            # on Azure: AzureOpenAI, set up as in bot.py
load_dotenv()                        # loads your keys from .env
client = OpenAI()
request_id = uuid.uuid4().hex[:8]    # this call's tracking number
t0 = time.perf_counter()
resp = client.chat.completions.create(
    model="gpt-4o-mini", temperature=0,       # on Azure: your deployment name
    messages=[{"role": "user", "content": "Say hello to Nimbus."}])
latency_ms = round((time.perf_counter() - t0) * 1000)

Print the reply, both token counts from resp.usage and latency_ms, then append them with the request_id as one JSON line to logs/runs.jsonl.

A hello-world call with the production habits built in: a request_id, a timer and a log line. time.perf_counter() is a high-resolution clock for timing; resp.usage holds prompt_tokens and completion_tokens (input and output).

Left for you: print the reply (resp.choices[0].message.content), the two token counts and latency_ms, then put them in a dict with the request_id and append it with json.dumps as one line to logs/runs.jsonl. Create the logs folder first. Done when each run adds exactly one line.

Stuck? "Key not found" usually means load_dotenv() didn't run. On Azure, use your deployment name, not the model name. checkpoints/01-triage/obslog.py shows the logging pattern.

This step makes the cost of the context window concrete. Build three prompts from T-1001's text: the ticket alone, plus shared/policies/refund-policy.md, plus all six policy files. Read the counts from resp.usage, then price each with the formula from the cost slide, using the PRICE_TABLE row for the model you actually called.

Expect roughly 60, 480 and 2,400 prompt tokens; a few extra is normal, because the API adds some tokens per message. Judge the last column honestly: for this ticket the refund policy helps, and the other five add cost and little else.

Bonus: tiktoken.get_encoding("o200k_base").encode(text) counts tokens for GPT-4o and GPT-4.1 models before you send anything.

Tonight · step 420 calls: cost, average, p95

  • Loop the same prompt 20 times, e.g. "Classify this ticket as refund, replacement or question: …" with T-1001. Log every call.
  • Compute the total cost and the average latency.
  • Compute p95: sorted(latencies)[18], the 19th of 20.
  • Compare: how much bigger is your p95 than your average?
  • Bonus: run 5 calls at temperature=1.0 asking for a one-sentence resolution. Are the 5 answers the same?

This step measures the slow tail on your own network. Time each of the 20 calls with perf_counter, log each one, and keep the latencies in a list. Total cost is the sum of each call's cost. For p95 with 20 values, sorted(latencies)[18] is the 19th value, because Python counts from 0. Divide p95 by the average to get your multiplier.

A good result: a p95 clearly above the average, and a multiplier that changes if you run it again later, since networks and time of day move the tail a lot. In the temperature bonus, the five answers almost always differ in wording and often in the decision: incident #8 on your own machine.
